If your Parker home has 100-amp service and a gas furnace, gas water heater, gas range, and no EV, you're probably fine — 100 amps covers that comfortably. You cross into needing 200-amp service when you start adding large electric loads: an EV charger, a hot tub, electric heat or a heat pump, a large electric range, a shop, or a second EV. Two of those at once on 100-amp service is usually the tipping point.

Here's how to work out where your house sits.

## How to read your current service

Look at the main breaker at the top of your [electrical panel](/electrical-panel-services/). The number stamped on it — 100, 125, 150, 200 — is your service size. If you have a fuse box instead of breakers, you're almost certainly at 100 amps or less and the panel is due regardless of the amperage question.

Then look at how full the panel is. A panel with every slot used, tandem "cheater" breakers doubled up, and handwritten labels crammed in the margin is telling you the house has outgrown it — sometimes before the amperage itself is the limit.

## What actually uses a lot of amps

Rough continuous draw, 240-volt loads:

| Load | Typical amps |
|---|---|
| Level 2 EV charger | 24–48 |
| Hot tub / spa | 30–50 |
| Electric range | 30–40 |
| Electric dryer | 24–30 |
| Central AC | 15–30 |
| Electric water heater | 20–25 |
| Heat pump / electric furnace | 20–60 |

A proper load calculation doesn't just add these up — it applies demand factors, because you never run all of them at full tilt simultaneously. That's why a real assessment matters more than a spreadsheet. An electrician runs the NEC calculation on your actual house and tells you whether 100 is genuinely maxed, whether 150 buys you enough headroom, or whether 200 is the right call.

## When 200-amp service is the clear answer

- You're adding an [EV charger](/residential-ev-charging/) and you already have central AC and an electric range
- You want two EVs
- You're going from gas to electric heat, or adding a heat pump
- You're finishing a basement with its own HVAC, kitchenette, or shop
- The panel is a Federal Pacific or Zinsco — you're replacing it anyway, so size up while you're there

## When a subpanel is the cheaper right answer

If your *service* has capacity but your *panel* is just out of physical space, a subpanel adds circuit slots without a full service upgrade. This is common in homes where the service is 150 or 200 amps but the original panel was small. It's a smaller job and a smaller bill. The catch: if the service itself is undersized, a subpanel doesn't help — you're still pulling from the same limited supply.

## What a 200-amp upgrade involves in Parker

Most panel upgrades in Parker run **$2,200–$4,500**. What moves the price: whether the meter and mast need replacing, where the panel is relative to the meter, the grounding and bonding the inspector will require, and whether the utility service drop needs upsizing.

The job itself is usually a single day. CORE Electric Cooperative (or Xcel, depending on your address) disconnects the power in the morning, the old panel comes out, the new one goes in with every circuit labelled, and power is back that afternoon. The Town of Parker or Douglas County inspects it, and the utility reconnects.

We pull the permit, schedule the utility, and meet the inspector — you don't chase any of that. If you're weighing an EV charger or a hot tub, get the [load calculation](/electrical-panel-services/) done first: if an upgrade is needed, it's the long-lead item, and it's better to know before the equipment shows up.
